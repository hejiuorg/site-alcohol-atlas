Hôtel-Dieu des Hospices de Beaune

A 15th-century charitable hospital in central Beaune, founded in 1443 by Nicolas Rolin, chancellor to the Duke of Burgundy, and his wife Guigone de Salins. It stopped taking patients in 1971 and now operates as a museum, known for its polychrome glazed-tile roofs, Rogier van der Weyden's Last Judgment polyptych and the annual November wine auction.

The Hôtel-Dieu des Hospices de Beaune stands in the old centre of Beaune at 2 Rue de l'Hôtel-Dieu. It is a 15th-century charitable hospital first and a museum second. Nicolas Rolin, chancellor to the Duke of Burgundy, and his wife Guigone de Salins founded it in 1443 as a "palace for the poor," receiving pilgrims, wanderers and patients who could not pay for care. It stopped taking patients in 1971, and the building was preserved as one of Burgundy's most visited historic sites. Its name also belongs to a hospital group that still operates, the Hospices Civils de Beaune, a point visitors often miss.

Place and history

In 1443 the Rolin couple funded the Hôtel-Dieu and placed it in the care of nursing sisters. The decision was both charitable and political: in the late years of the Hundred Years' War the Burgundian court needed to display order and faith, and a hospital for the poor served that purpose well. The institution continued for nearly six centuries through wars, fires, financial crises and changes in medicine, while the building and its collections survived in unusually complete form. The turning point came in 1971, when the Hôtel-Dieu stopped admitting patients and moved into the museum and heritage system; ticket income and donations now support other facilities in the hospital group.

The link with wine is not accidental. The Hospices Civils de Beaune also owns vineyards, originally donated and later expanded through purchases and exchanges. In 1859 the hospital held its first public sale of the year's wine, and the event became an annual charity auction held on the third Sunday of November. The proceeds support the hospital and charitable work, while the sale has become a fixed point in the Burgundy wine calendar. The distinction matters: the Hôtel-Dieu is a museum, and the wine auction belongs to the hospital group's wine estate. They share the name "Hospices de Beaune" but are not the same visitor product.

Spaces and displays

The Hôtel-Dieu is arranged around a courtyard, and its most striking feature is the polychrome glazed-tile roof: red, brown, yellow and green tiles form geometric patterns, a signature of hospital architecture in the Burgundy–Flanders region. One side of the courtyard is the Salle des Pôvres, the paupers' ward, where long beds line the walls under a timber roof shaped like an inverted ship's hull. This is not a display about the prehistory of the modern hospital ward; it shows directly how charitable care was organised through space.

The most important object is Rogier van der Weyden's Last Judgment polyptych. Commissioned by Rolin and originally made for the hospital chapel, it is now displayed in the Hôtel-Dieu; judgment, charity and the donors' names are brought into one visual system, making it the key work for understanding the hospital's religious, charitable and political relationships. The kitchen, pharmacy, bloodletting and surgical instruments, tapestries, furniture and donor portraits complete a route in which medicine and daily life are intertwined. In 2026 the museum's annual theme, "MOUVEMENT(S)," organises talks, workshops and evening events around bodies, care and architectural change.

Pay attention to the scale of the display. The Hôtel-Dieu is not a large encyclopaedic museum; it is a preserved building complex in which the relation between objects and rooms matters more than any single treasure. Do not photograph the courtyard roof and leave. The paupers' ward, pharmacy and chapel are what connect "hospital" to "museum." If wine interests you, the displays mention the vineyards and the auction, but do not treat the place as a tasting room. Its subject is hospital heritage, not cellars.

How to visit

There are two ways in: self-guided visits and guided tours. Self-guided visitors follow the route set by the museum and pick up orientation material at the entrance. Guided tours run at fixed times and their themes change with the annual programme; in 2026 "MOUVEMENT(S)" links medical, architectural and care history. Tickets and guided tours are bought through the official booking platform by time slot, and advance booking is sensible at weekends and in high season. A ticket desk may have space on the day, but that cannot be assumed.

The Hôtel-Dieu does not offer regular wine tastings. Auction week in November brings wine-related events and dinners, but those are separate arrangements, not part of an ordinary museum ticket. If you want to see the hospital and drink wine on the same day, put the Hôtel-Dieu in the morning and a winery visit in the afternoon, leaving time for lunch and travel. A visit usually takes one to two hours; a guided tour adds more time and suits travellers with a specific interest in medical history, architecture or medieval art.

Practical information

Address: 2 Rue de l'Hôtel-Dieu, 21200 Beaune, France (coordinates 47.02194, 4.83667). The hospital is in the core of old Beaune, close to Place de la Halle.

Getting there: from Beaune railway station (Gare de Beaune, on TER Bourgogne lines), walk into the old town — about ten minutes to the Hôtel-Dieu; a TER ticket less than 48 hours old qualifies for a discount on admission. By car, leave the A6/E60 motorway at exit 24.0 or 24.1 and use the Louis Véry car park, then walk into the pedestrian zone; the old streets are narrow, so in high season it is better to park outside the centre than to drive to the entrance.

Opening hours (2026): 1 January–31 March and 16 November–31 December, 9:00–12:30 and 14:00–18:30 (on 1 January and 25 December only 14:00–18:30); 1 April–15 November, 9:00–19:30. Last entry is one hour before closing; public holidays, temporary closures and special events can still change this, so check the official booking platform before setting out.

Tickets: adult €12.50, ages 6–17 €6.25, students (under 26) and other concessions €9.50; guided tours are booked by time slot and priced separately on the official booking platform. Advance booking is sensible at weekends and in high season; a ticket desk may have space on the day, but that cannot be assumed. A visit usually takes one to two hours, comfortable shoes matter more than formal dress, and some rooms are dimly lit, so turn off the flash when taking photographs.

Etiquette and common mistakes

The first mistake is to treat "Hospices de Beaune" as a winery. It does own vineyards and holds an auction, but the Hôtel-Dieu itself is a museum; outside auction week there is no permanent tasting. The second mistake is to treat the hospital as an abandoned building. It no longer admits patients, but it operates as the heritage institution of a hospital group, with maintenance, research and education continuing; visitors should treat it as a working cultural institution.

The third mistake is to look only at the coloured roof. The roof deserves attention, but the Last Judgment polyptych, the paupers' ward and the pharmacy are the clues to why the hospital matters. The fourth is to imagine the auction as a walk-in tourist event. It takes place on the third Sunday of November and requires registration or access through official channels; most visitors watch the festivities rather than bid in the sale room.

Keep quiet, especially in the chapel and the paupers' ward, and do not touch tapestries, furniture or medical instruments. Families can use "how the hospital cared for the poor" as a thread for children rather than turning the ward into a haunted house. If you visit during auction week, central Beaune is extremely crowded and restaurants and parking need to be arranged in advance. Those days are about a city festival, not a quiet museum visit, and expectations should be separated.

Around and routes

The Hôtel-Dieu sits in the core of old Beaune and works well in a half-day walk with Place de la Halle, the Collégiale Notre-Dame, the Beaune belfry and the town walls. See the hospital first in the morning before the groups arrive, have lunch near the market, then walk to the cellars of Joseph Drouhin in the afternoon or drive to Château de Meursault in Meursault, about 15 minutes away. If the wine auction interests you, start at the Marché aux Vins to understand the Burgundy merchant system, then return to the hospital for its auction history.

For a one-day visit, put the Hôtel-Dieu in the morning and a winery in the afternoon. Old Beaune is compact, but the museum is information-dense, and two cellar visits in a row can be tiring; a lunch and a walk in between make the day much better. The Hôtel-Dieu and Château de Meursault are both stops on the Burgundy wine route, but the former tells a story of charity, medicine and the city, while the latter tells a story of parcels, cellars and cuvées. They are not the same kind of "winery visit."
