The Brazen Head

A historic Dublin pub on Lower Bridge Street; the house traces a hostelry on the site to 1198 and dates the present building to 1754, and it still serves Irish food, stout and traditional music.

How the house tells its own story

If you want a Dublin pub that can explain the word "old" without turning into a theme park, the Brazen Head is a sensible place to start. It stands at 20 Lower Bridge Street, beside Father Mathew Bridge over the Liffey, on the spot where Dublin's original river crossing is said to have been. The house traces its opening to 1198 and calls itself "Ireland's oldest pub"; the building you actually face, however, is a coaching inn rebuilt in 1754. Keeping those two dates apart is the key to understanding the place. What it sells is not a medieval room but a site that has kept receiving travellers and citizens for centuries. It still opens every day, serves Irish home cooking by day and runs traditional music sessions at night, with tourists and locals sharing the same bar.

From the ford to the 1754 building

Dublin's Irish name, Baile Átha Cliath, means "the town of the ford of the reed hurdles", and it refers to the shallow crossing in this part of the Liffey. The house says reed matting was once laid on the riverbed so travellers could cross safely at low tide; Father Mathew Bridge stands near that old crossing. The pub claims a hostelry has stood here since 1198, the name "The Brazen Head" appears in documents by 1653, and the present building was put up in 1754 as a coaching inn. A 1750s advertisement mentions that Christopher Quinn of the Brazen Head in Bridge Street had fitted the house with "neat accommodations and commodious cellars".

The 1198 date belongs to the house's own tradition and to common local storytelling; it cannot be checked against a continuous run of contemporary trading records. That is why "Ireland's oldest pub" is best treated as a well-founded but not absolute title. A recent study listed the Brazen Head as the fifth oldest pub in the world, and the house happily cites it, but such rankings tend to rely on start dates supplied by the pubs themselves and do not use a single method. For a traveller, the safer reading is this: a site with a long documentary record and centuries of hospitality, not a twelfth-century building preserved intact. Set it inside the wider history of taverns and the difference between an old site and an old building becomes obvious.

Rooms, music and the list

Inside, you find low ceilings, stone walls, dark wood and a warren of connected small rooms; corridors are narrow and turn unexpectedly, so a first visit can involve getting lost between the bar and the back rooms. The front bar is the place to linger: the traditional sessions usually happen there, with musicians sitting among the guests rather than on a raised stage. The official site has listed a 12:00 opening, food until 21:00 from Sunday to Thursday and until 21:30 on Friday and Saturday, with music from 21:00 and an extra Sunday afternoon session. These arrangements change with the season and with events, so check official channels before you go.

The list is built around beer, above all Guinness and Irish stout. The bar pulls a pint in the standard two-stage pour, letting the head settle before topping it up. You will usually also find Irish red ale, cider, whiskey and Irish coffee. Food is pub home cooking: Irish stew, beef and Guinness stew, fish and chips, pies and sandwiches are all common, but the menu changes, so do not order from an old guide. This is not a craft-beer bar, and you do not need a long list of style names. A local stout and a hot plate is the most natural way to use the place.

Getting there today

The address is 20 Lower Bridge St, Usher's Quay, Dublin 8, D08 WC64, Ireland, on the south bank of the Liffey just south of Father Mathew Bridge. From Trinity College or the Temple Bar area it is a 15- to 20-minute walk; from Dublin Castle, Christ Church Cathedral or St Patrick's Cathedral it is closer. On public transport, get off at the Four Courts or Jervis stop on the Luas Red Line and walk for about five to eight minutes; several buses run along Usher's Quay and Merchant's Quay. Driving in central Dublin is slow and parking is expensive, so a car is not advisable.

The pub fits neatly into a half-day route on the west side of the old city: see Christ Church and St Patrick's, walk along the Liffey to Lower Bridge Street, have a drink, then continue to the Guinness Storehouse or back towards Temple Bar. On weekend evenings and in high season there can be a queue and group bookings at the door; if you want to look at the building in peace, a weekday afternoon is better than a night. Accessibility is limited: the doorway and internal passages of an old building are uneven, so wheelchair users and families with pushchairs should telephone ahead.

Ordering and house etiquette

The default rule in an Irish pub is to order at the bar and then find a seat; do not sit down and wait for table service. When it is your turn, be clear: "a pint of Guinness, please" gets you a pint, while "a glass" or "a half pint" gets you a smaller measure. If several people are ordering together, the bartender will usually set out all the drinks before taking payment, so do not keep cutting back in one drink at a time. Dublin bars generally accept cards, but a little cash helps in small or very busy places. Tipping is not expected at the bar; when you eat at a table, rounding up or leaving about ten per cent is common.

A music session is not a stage show. The musicians sit in a circle in a corner, and you can applaud between tunes, but do not talk loudly through the playing, use a flash or film constantly, or keep calling out requests. Before taking photographs, look at whether people are listening closely; the simplest way to support the players is to buy a drink, listen quietly and, at the end, buy a CD or merchandise of their own. The Brazen Head is also a local pub, so do not treat the front room as a tour-group assembly point; large groups should contact the house in advance to check whether it can take them.

Common mistakes and walking range

The first mistake is to treat 1198 as the date of the building: the standing fabric is an eighteenth-century coaching inn, while 1198 refers to the tradition of hospitality on the site. The second is to present "Ireland's oldest" as a settled fact; it is more accurate to say that the house and local storytelling commonly call it so. The third is to expect traditional music all day; in practice it is concentrated in the evening, with an extra Sunday afternoon session. The fourth is to treat the place as a drinking museum: the kitchen works normally and many people come for dinner.

Nearby you can walk to Father Mathew Bridge, the Four Courts and the Liffey quays; east lie Dublin Castle and Temple Bar, south are Christ Church and St Patrick's Cathedrals, and west is the Guinness Storehouse. To put the Irish pub into a wider urban context, read Pub and city; for the country's beer and tavern tradition, read Ireland. The Brazen Head works best as one stop on a route rather than the only destination.
