American Bar at The Savoy

The classic American Bar inside The Savoy in London, opened in 1893 and known for The Savoy Cocktail Book, Ada Coleman's Hanky Panky and its Art Deco room.

Few bars in London treat continuity as a selling point the way the American Bar does. It opened with The Savoy in 1893, survived wars, changes of ownership and repeated refurbishments, and the hotel still describes it as London's longest continuously operating cocktail bar. Visitors often hear that sentence and picture a perfectly preserved old room. The more useful reading is that this is a hotel service space first: its pace, prices and manners exist to make a planned evening go well, not to reconstruct a historical scene.

Place and history

The Savoy hotel opened in 1889, and the American Bar followed in 1893. That gap matters. The bar was not an afterthought attached to a lobby; it was part of the hotel's attempt to bring American-style cocktail culture into London society. In the early twentieth century, Ada Coleman became head bartender. Regulars called her "Coley," and she held the post for 23 years. One of her drinks was made for the actor Charles Hawtrey, who reportedly asked for something with more punch; Coleman combined gin, sweet vermouth and a few dashes of Fernet-Branca, and Hawtrey's alleged remark about "the real hanky-panky" gave the cocktail its name. The Hanky Panky is still served today, and the hotel's own account says it uses vintage gin, vintage sweet vermouth and vintage Fernet-Branca.

Harry Craddock, Coleman's successor, published The Savoy Cocktail Book in 1930. The book turned a working bar's repertoire into a written canon, which is why the American Bar's importance is not only about famous guests. It helped standardise how cocktails were recorded, taught and copied. Many drinks now treated as classics can be traced to that moment of documentation, and the bar's reputation as a school as much as a stage comes from the same source.

The room and the list

The bar sits on the ground floor of the hotel, close to the lobby and the Strand entrance. The room is not large. Mirrors, warm wood, geometric lines and a piano create a restrained theatricality, and the movement of the service team is more revealing than the furniture. The Art Deco atmosphere here is a maintained language rather than a guarantee that every object dates from the 1920s. Treating the room as a design museum will lead to disappointment; treating it as a working hotel bar will not.

The menu normally runs along three lines: canonical recipes, house cocktails named after Savoy guests and staff, and a vintage or rare spirits list. In November 2026 the official page cited "Since 1986," a tribute to doorman Tony Cortegaca; "Painter's Palette," inspired by Monet's 1899 stay at the hotel; and "Red Carpet," drawn from a 1950 Dior show held at The Savoy. Names change with the menu, but the structure is stable: a familiar story is offered first, then turned into a drink for the present. Hanky Panky and White Lady do the other job, which is to calibrate your palate against the bar's own history.

How to drink

For a first visit, the safest pattern is one classic plus one current house drink. Start with a Hanky Panky or a White Lady to test the basics, then ask the bartender for a house creation that suits your taste. Sitting at the bar lets you watch the shaking, straining and garnishing, but bar seats are limited and become scarce after dinner. If your group wants to talk, take a table.

Do not order a long run of cocktails at once. The drinks are generous in alcohol and sugar, and the differences only appear when you slow down. State your preferences before the bartender starts: dry or sweet, gin or whisky, carbonated or stirred. Handing over "something strong" usually produces a drink you may not want to finish.

Practical information

The address is The Savoy, Strand, London WC2R 0EZ, United Kingdom. The bar is on the ground floor; enter through Savoy Court. The nearest Underground stations are Embankment (District, Circle and Northern lines) and Charing Cross (Northern and Bakerloo lines, plus National Rail), both about a five-minute walk; Temple is about seven minutes on foot. Savoy Court is one of the few private lanes in London where traffic is directed to drive on the right, so pedestrians should not stand in the roadway for photographs.

The official page in November 2026 listed Monday to Saturday 12:00–24:00 and Sunday 12:00–22:00, with a minimum age of 16. The dress code is "elegant"; sportswear and football shirts are likely to be refused. Book through the hotel's official channel (the website links to SevenRooms), especially at peak times. Prices sit in the upper tier of London hotel bars, far above an ordinary pub, and service is added. The menu changes by season, and hours may be adjusted on holidays and event days, so check the day's official notice. Opening status, prices and bookings are subject to official confirmation. Etiquette and common mistakes

The first mistake is reading "London's longest continuously operating cocktail bar" as "London's oldest room." Continuity comes from a hotel system and a service team, not from a single surviving plank of wood. The second is dressing as if this were a daytime shopping trip; the rule is simple, but "elegant" is enforced in practice. Third, do not arrive at peak hour with a long list of off-menu experiments. The bartenders are willing to talk, but the room has a rhythm to protect. Keep other guests out of your photographs, and do not take loud calls during the piano set. Finally, this is not a pub: there is no standing at the door with a pint, no shouting across the room, and no taking your glass into the street.

Around and routes

From Embankment station, walk along the Thames to Savoy Court in about five minutes. After a drink you can reach Somerset House in five to eight minutes, Covent Garden in about ten, or continue along the river to Waterloo Bridge. Theatregoers often use the bar as a pre-show stop for the Savoy Theatre and return afterwards, which makes the interval and post-show periods crowded. To understand the difference between a hotel bar and a neighbourhood pub, visit a Covent Garden pub in the afternoon and come back here in the evening; the contrast in pace is clearer than any description.
