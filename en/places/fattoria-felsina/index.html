Fattoria Fèlsina

A family estate at Castelnuovo Berardenga, east of Siena, bought by the Poggiali family in 1966; known for Sangiovese clones, Rancia and Fontalloro, tufa cellars and olive oil, and a representative estate on the south-eastern edge of Chianti Classico.

East of Siena, the hills gradually shift from the Black Rooster country of Chianti Classico to the bare earth and wheat fields of the Crete Senesi. Fattoria Fèlsina sits on the border between the two. It is not on the main Gaiole or Radda tourist circuit and does not rely on a castle or tower to attract visitors. It looks more like a working agricultural estate: vineyards, olive groves, woodland, farmhouses and cellars form a single organism, with the entrance just outside Castelnuovo Berardenga.

An estate on a boundary

The official description places Fèlsina on the "land border between Chianti and the Crete Senesi". This is not a figure of speech. To the north lie the vineyards and oak woods of Chianti Classico; to the south, the clay and exposed hills of the Crete. The vineyards are spread across historic farms, including Rancia, Rancino, Arcidosso, Arcidossino/Santa Letizia, Casale di Fèlsina, Fèlsina, Casalino, Santa Maria, San Giuseppe, Ruzzatoio, Molino d'Ombrone, Valli, Molinuzzo and Terra Rossa. Those names still appear in plot management and in the wines' stories.

Fèlsina is not a newly built winery. The official account says the farms have histories going back more than a thousand years; after the Second World War, sharecropping families left the countryside and the estate structure fragmented. When the Poggiali family bought the property in 1966, it was still an agricultural estate rather than a modern wine brand.

The visit begins at the Bottega, the wine shop and small restaurant, and can then move into the vineyards and cellars. The official advice is to separate the visit, the tasting and the shop: first understand the land and the process, then sit down to drink, and only then decide what to buy.

From 1966 to Rancia and Fontalloro

In 1966 Domenico Poggiali bought Fèlsina at a time when Italian viticulture was struggling. The official account calls it a courageous leap: rather than dividing and selling the land, he invested in wine quality and a young team. Within a few years the vineyards had grown to more than forty hectares, and the organisation of the estate changed with them. In the second half of the 1970s Giuseppe Mazzocolin joined the family business to lead commercial development.

1983 was the key year: the first Rancia and Fontalloro were made. Rancia comes from the vineyard of the same name and is now a Chianti Classico Riserva; Fontalloro comes from vineyards that cross the boundary of Chianti Classico and is bottled as a Tuscan IGT. One stayed inside the appellation, the other used the IGT to keep more freedom in blending and ageing. Understanding this explains how Fèlsina can speak both inside and outside the appellation rules.

From 1990, Giuseppe's eldest son Giovanni Poggiali joined the estate and pushed modernisation with his father. In 1998 the winery introduced stainless-steel fermentation equipment, giving the winemaker and cellar master more precise control of temperature and extraction. The official account insists that tradition was not replaced: the heart of the cellar remains the old space cut into the tufa hillside.

The vineyards: a library of Sangiovese clones

The most important thing to see at Fèlsina is not a building but the Sangiovese in the vineyards. The official account says the estate has long carried out mass selection, preserving a large number of Sangiovese clones and rootstock combinations, giving the blend an infinite palette of nuances. The team also studies soil type, altitude and exposure, vinifies plots separately and then decides which go into the annata, which into Riserva and which into Gran Selezione.

This approach explains the "vertical" quality of Fèlsina's wines: the same variety, across different plots, clones and vintages, can show red fruit, flowers, spice, leather and tobacco in different proportions. If a member of staff takes you into the vineyard, look at row spacing, vine vigour, soil colour and stones rather than listening only for the words "organic" or "biodynamic". The official account says the estate remains open to experimental and biodynamic approaches, but whether certification exists and which plots it covers should be checked with the winery.

The estate also grows olives. Since 2002 it has run the "Oil according to Veronelli" project, matching olive varieties to plots. The main varieties are Pendolino, Leccino, Moraiolo and Raggiolo (Correggiolo), and the oil is bottled separately from three production areas: Fèlsina, Pagliaresi and Boschi.

The cellars and the wines

Fèlsina's cellars are cut into the tufa hillside. When Domenico Poggiali took over in 1966, he found a small stone cellar with a wide brick vault. In the early 1970s the estate built an underground cellar connecting to the old stables, which is still used for ageing. The old stable spaces were kept, and the barrels, vaults and temperature make "ageing" a place you can walk through rather than a number on a label.

The wines can be read along four lines. The first is Berardenga: the Chianti Classico annata and Riserva, the estate's everyday face, with more direct fruit, acidity and tannin. The second is Rancia: the Chianti Classico Riserva from the farm of the same name, tighter in structure and longer-lived, Fèlsina's flagship inside the appellation. The third is Colonia: the Chianti Classico Gran Selezione, the estate's answer to the top tier. The fourth is Fontalloro: a Tuscan IGT from vineyards that cross the Chianti Classico boundary, freer in style and often compared with Rancia.

There are also Maestro Raro (IGT), I Sistri (white), Nero di Nubi, Vin Santo and sparkling wines. On a first visit, compare the Berardenga annata, Rancia and Fontalloro: the first for everyday expression, the second for Riserva structure, the third for IGT freedom. If the budget allows, add a glass of Colonia to understand Gran Selezione's concentration.

Visits and practical information

The address is Via del Chianti 101, 53019 Castelnuovo Berardenga (SI), Italy; the official coordinates are 43.349499, 11.501247. By car it is about thirty minutes from Siena, an hour and fifteen minutes from Florence and thirty minutes from Gaiole in Chianti. The estate entrance is just outside Castelnuovo Berardenga; follow the "Fèlsina" signs. Public transport is infrequent, so a car, a hired driver or a booked taxi is the practical choice.

The usual hours for visits and the wine shop are Monday to Sunday 10:00–18:00; the estate is closed on Sundays in January and February and on 25–26 December and 1 January. Tours and tastings should be booked in advance by email at welcome@felsina.it or by phone on +39 0577 1523789; you can also ask at the Bottega whether a place is available that day. Visits usually include the vineyards and cellars, with tastings at different levels; prices, duration and language are confirmed by the winery.

The Bottega serves light meals and sells wine and olive oil, making it convenient before or after a visit. If you are combining Brolio Castle and Antinori in one day, Fèlsina works better in the afternoon: it is closer to Siena, and the return to Siena or Florence is straightforward after the visit. Etiquette, surroundings and the route

The estate is a working agricultural and production site, not a park. Do not enter closed vineyards, climb fences or touch fermentation tanks and barrels; cellar floors can be wet, so wear shoes with grip. Smell before tasting, sip slowly and spit what you do not want; avoid strong perfume, which interferes with other visitors' reading of Sangiovese. Families with children should say so in advance, as some tastings are not suitable for minors.

Nearby you can continue to Castelnuovo Berardenga, the historic centre of Siena and the landscape roads of the Crete Senesi; to the north, Gaiole and Brolio Castle; to the west, Siena. If you have time for only one stop, Fèlsina suits anyone who wants to taste Sangiovese seriously; if you want castles and architecture, Brolio is the better choice. The two are about thirty minutes apart by car, but do not drive after a tasting: a designated driver or a hired car is the precondition for this route.
