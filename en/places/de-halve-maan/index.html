A brewery that still brews in central Bruges

De Halve Maan stands at Walplein 26 in the old centre of Bruges, postcode 8000, beside the Begijnhof and fifteen minutes on foot from the station. It still brews on the original site: mash, fermenters, bottling line and visitor route share one building, and the door opens onto a cobbled square of cafes.

It fits a walking day: the Begijnhof first, the tour next, then the canals towards the Markt. Against Cantillon in Brussels, it shows the other Belgian branch: top-fermenting ale rather than spontaneous lambic, less than an hour away by train.

Six generations, two brands

Brewing has taken place here for more than five centuries, but the family story starts in 1856, when Leon Maes, also called Henri the First, became owner and brewed to his own recipe; his first beer was hazy and sour. In 1867 Henri the Second and his brother Achère brought back English techniques then among the most modern. Both died young, and their widows kept the brewery rather than sell it.

In 1919 Henri the Third invested in new equipment and began bottom fermentation; in the thirties he made a bock and a popular pale lager. In 1981 Véronique Maes, fifth generation, joined her father Henri the Fourth and launched Straffe Hendrik, "Strong Hendrik", after the family's many Henris. In 2005 the sixth generation, Xavier Vanneste, launched Brugse Zot and secured the brewery's future.

The tour: 220 steps and a rooftop

Two tours run. The classic lasts about 45 minutes for at most 25 people, includes one beer and costs 16 EUR; under sixes are free and ages 7 to 15 pay 8.50 EUR, in Dutch, French or English. The XL lasts about 90 minutes, includes three beers, costs 26 EUR and runs at weekends in Dutch and English. Both include 220 steps, some steep, and are not suited to visitors with limited mobility.

The route starts in the brewhouse, passes equipment from several generations and ends on the roof with a 360-degree view. In April 2024 it was renewed with a virtual experience about the pipeline and bottling plant. It closes with a tasting, the only place where unfiltered Brugse Zot and Straffe Hendrik are poured. More than 100,000 people visit yearly, so book ahead.

The beers: Brugse Zot and Straffe Hendrik

Brugse Zot Blond is the flagship, brewed with four malts and two aromatic hops, golden with balanced fruit and malt. Brugse Zot Dubbel is darker, with caramel and dark fruit. Straffe Hendrik Tripel is drier and stronger with a bitter finish; the Quadrupel is full-bodied, and Heritage is oak-aged in limited quantities. Blanche de Bruges is a wheat beer, Brugse Bok a seasonal bock, Rosé de Bruges carries fruit, and Sportzot is the alcohol-free speciality launched in 2018.

All belong to the top-fermenting family of beer: warm fermentation produces esters, followed by cold conditioning. Belgian brewing allows sugar and syrup to raise strength and adjust body, so the power of a Tripel is not only malt. Style names have no single legal definition; check strength and description rather than the label alone.

The 3.3 km pipeline: replacing trucks with gravity

In 2016 the brewery completed a 3.3 km underground pipeline linking the city-centre brewhouse to its bottling plant outside the centre. Part of the cost came from crowdfunding, and the motive was practical: old-town streets are narrow and tankers are noisy and hard on the roads. Sending beer underground reduces traffic and keeps brewing in the centre, where every litre is still made.

The pipeline is the most memorable part of the tour because it joins tradition and engineering. For the city it is a tourism narrative; for the brewery it is a compromise between capacity and location, typical of Bruges, where heritage and industry must coexist.

Belgian styles and the edge of regulation

Belgium has no German-style purity law, and brewers may use sugar, spices and fruit. That freedom widens the range but makes "style" a matter of convention. Tripel, Dubbel and Quadrupel are not legal grades, and Blanche is not a fixed recipe; the label with strict protection is Trappist, reserved for specific monasteries. De Halve Maan is a family brewery, so its beers are commercial interpretations of abbey-style brewing, not Trappist beer.

Recipes and strengths change, so read the bottle. Comparing Belgian top fermentation with lambic, the two breweries share a treatment of fermentation as the source of flavour rather than a step to be neutralised, which is part of why Belgium resists a single beer story.

Practical information

Address: Walplein 26, 8000 Brugge, Belgium. Getting there: 15 minutes on foot from Bruges station; cobbles are hard on wheeled luggage, and the brewery has no car park, so use a public one at the edge of the centre. Hours: tours are sold by slot, with several classic tours daily and the XL at weekends; restaurant and tavern hours differ. Prices: 16 EUR classic, 26 EUR XL, both including beer; child and group rates are online.

Notes: 220 steps, so wear shoes with grip; cellars and stairs are narrow in high season; do not start on an empty stomach; respect the legal drinking age. Central Bruges hotels are expensive and staying near the station is practical. This entry does not list coordinates; use official channels for those.
