Provence rosé trail: Taradeau to Correns

From Château de Selle in Taradeau to Miraval in Correns, two stops about an hour apart. One day sets two Provençal rosé paths side by side: a working cellar that receives visitors by appointment, and a private estate that stays closed and sells only through channels. The drive also explains the Mistral, limestone and schist, and the direct-press method behind pale rosé.

This route has two stops: Château de Selle in Taradeau and Miraval in Correns. Both are in Provence, both make pale rosé, and they represent two entirely different ways of organising a wine business. The first belongs to the three-estate Domaines Ott group, and its cellar and tasting room open on weekdays by appointment; the second is a private estate that the official FAQ says is closed to the public, with wines sold only through Famille Perrin's tasting room and authorised channels. Linking them in one day is not about visiting more cellars; it is about where the line between "visit" and "no visit" falls in the same region.

One hour between two stops

Put Taradeau first, because it is somewhere you can actually enter. Château de Selle is at 5093 Route de Flayosc, 83460 Taradeau; from Les Baux-de-Provence or Aix it is a little over an hour by car. The second stop lies east in Correns, at Miraval, 83570 Correns, about an hour from Taradeau. The route suits a car: public transport in the inland Var is sparse, and neither estate sits near a station.

The order can be reversed — see the Correns valley and terraces first, then return to Taradeau for an afternoon tasting. With only one day, however, put the bookable cellar in the morning and the closed estate in the afternoon, so that a long first stop cannot make you miss the appointment. The route has no legal boundary; it is simply a driving line defined by two real places.

How the Mistral rewrites a vineyard

The Mistral is unavoidable in any Provençal wine story. It blows south from the Rhône valley towards the Mediterranean, dry, steady and sometimes violent. It removes humidity and reduces fungal pressure, leaving healthier fruit; it can also tear young shoots and strip flowers, forcing growers to spend more on training, tying and wind protection. In a windy year the vineyard does not look romantic; it looks unusually tidy.

For rosé the real effect is cleaner ripening. Low humidity keeps the fruit surface dry and makes the harvest window easier to judge; cool nights help keep acidity. The CIVP describes Provence as three appellations within one system, but every valley has its own wind path, exposure and altitude. Neither Taradeau nor Correns is coastal, so direct sea breeze matters little; inland day-to-night range and valley airflow do the work.

Limestone and schist: two subsoils

CIVP material divides the subsoil of Côtes de Provence into two broad systems: limestone in the north and west, and crystalline rock, schist and volcanic rock in the south and east. Limestone drains quickly, forcing roots to find water deeper down, and often gives fruit with clear acidity and a mineral edge. Schist and crystalline rock break up differently, holding and warming water in their own way, and usually produce a broader body. Place both in one route and the difference in the glass is geology, not just house style.

Château de Selle at Taradeau belongs to the limestone side, with gypsum, sandstone and red clay in its vineyards. The CIVP splits the whole appellation into eight production basins and recognises five complementary geographical denominations: Cru Sainte-Victoire, Fréjus, La Londe, Pierrefeu and Notre-Dame des Anges, added in 2019. These names sit in the rules of the appellation, not on a marketing wish-list. Understanding them is more useful than remembering that Provence is sunny.

Direct pressing, short maceration, pale colour

The colour of rosé comes from contact between juice and skins. The dominant method in Provence is direct pressing: fruit is pressed soon after harvest, so the two meet only briefly before a cool fermentation. The result is light salmon, grey-pink or onion-skin, with citrus, white flowers and redcurrant, dry on the palate. This is not at odds with short maceration: the shorter the maceration, the paler the colour, the lower the tannin, and the more the wine depends on balance between acidity and fruit.

Timing is critical. An early pick gives high acidity but thin aromas; a late pick raises alcohol and deepens colour. CIVP figures show that about 51% of the Côtes de Provence vineyard is certified organic or HVE, which suggests that light extraction is not only an aesthetic choice but part of a wider approach to inputs and clean fruit. In the tasting room, try a simple exercise: look at the depth of colour, then smell, then judge whether the mid-palate has weight. Pale is not the same as thin; what fails is a wine with a hollow middle.

Reading the Côtes de Provence hierarchy

Côtes de Provence became an AOP in 1977. Its vineyards cover more than 20,000 hectares across the Var, the Bouches-du-Rhône and one commune in the Alpes-Maritimes, 84 communes in all. It is not one uniform place but a map of eight production basins. "Hierarchy" in Provence is not only about estate rank: first check that the wine is Côtes de Provence, then whether it carries a complementary geographical denomination, and only then the estate's own historical titles such as Cru Classé.

The complementary denominations require tighter boundaries and stricter rules on yields and ripeness, so different parcels of one estate can fall into different names. "Côtes de Provence" on a label is the baseline; "Cru Sainte-Victoire" or "Fréjus" is a more specific geographical promise. When buying, read the appellation line as well as the estate name; when a brand blends across denominations, the label tells you which level the finished wine belongs to.

Stop one: the Taradeau cellar

Domaines Ott acquired its first Provençal estate, Château de Selle, in 1912. Today it is the most visitor-friendly of the three properties: the tasting room and cellar open Monday to Friday, 9:30–12:00 and 13:00–16:30, with a tour of about 45 minutes that must be booked. The cellar uses gravity flow and small vats matched to parcels, with stainless steel, oak foudres and spherical ceramic containers all in use.

The value of this stop is seeing the technique. Compare fermentation vessels of different size and material, and you begin to understand why the same limestone soil can produce rosés and reds of different shapes. Start with the Château de Selle rosé, then try Clos Mireille or Château Romassan to build a horizontal coordinate across the three terroirs. The cellar is normally closed at weekends, so do not place this stop on a Saturday.

Stop two: Miraval's boundary

Château Miraval in Correns covers more than 600 hectares, with vineyards on dry-stone terraces at an average altitude of about 350 metres. Its rosé is a Côtes de Provence wine, pale and dry, but the official FAQ states that the estate is not open to the public and that the wines are sold at Famille Perrin's tasting room in Châteauneuf-du-Pape. What you can see here is the valley and the appellation setting, not a cellar tasting.

This is not a temporary closure but the current arrangement. A private residence, working areas and vineyards share the same land, so opening it is impractical. Put it into the route as a lesson in brand and channel, not as a cellar visit. If you want to taste the wine, look for the Famille Perrin room or authorised retailers; if you want to enter a cellar, return to stop one or book another estate that genuinely opens.

Driving, booking and season

The two stops are about an hour apart, and with the drive in and out of Les Baux-de-Provence, two stops in a day is not rushed. Aim for Taradeau at 9:30 or 10:00, allow about an hour and a half for the tour and tasting, have lunch in a village on the way, then see Correns in the afternoon before driving back. In the reverse direction, remember that the cellar closes at 16:30 and allow at least an hour between the stops.

May to September is the most reliable season, but August afternoons are hot and neither the vineyards nor the cellar offer much shade; spring and autumn are more comfortable and busier with harvest events. On a strong Mistral day, outdoor stops shorten quickly. When booking, confirm whether tasting is included, whether children are welcome, the meeting point and the cancellation policy; inside the production area, do not touch cask racks or pipes.

Anyone driving should not drink at all, or should spit every sample. Provençal country roads are narrow and winding, and low afternoon sun hurts visibility; a designated driver, a hired car or a tour with transport is the safe arrangement. Opening hours, bookings and prices are subject to official channels; this route was verified on 2026-11-09.
